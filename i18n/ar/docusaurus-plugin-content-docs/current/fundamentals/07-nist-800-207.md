# Zero Trust Frameworks

Security frameworks are how organizations agree on what good looks like. When a job description asks for "zero trust experience" or an audit asks how access is controlled, the answer is usually expressed in the language of a published framework: NIST SP 800-207 for zero trust architecture, ISO 27001 for information security management, and NIST SP 800-63 for authentication strength. This page maps those frameworks to the specific practices covered elsewhere on this site, so the theory in one document and the how-to in another line up.

## NIST SP 800-207: the seven tenets of zero trust

[NIST SP 800-207](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/207/final) (final version, August 2020) defines zero trust architecture around seven tenets. They are the closest thing the industry has to an agreed definition of zero trust, and each one maps to a concrete practice:

| # | Tenet (paraphrased) | What it means in practice | Where this site covers it |
|---|---------------------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| 1 | All data sources and computing services are considered resources | There is no trusted "inside". Every device, API, and database is a resource with its own access policy, including printers and IoT gear | [Least Privilege & Zero Trust](04-least-privilege-zero-trust.md) |
| 2 | All communication is secured regardless of network location | Network location alone grants nothing; encryption and authentication apply equally on the LAN and over the internet | [Zero Trust IAM](../pam-core/06-zero-trust-iam.md) |
| 3 | Access to individual resources is granted on a per-session basis | Authentication for one resource does not automatically authorize another; sessions are scoped and short-lived | [Just-in-Time Access](../pam-core/07-jit-access.md) |
| 4 | Access is determined by dynamic policy | Policy evaluates identity, device posture, behavior, and environment at request time, not at enrollment time | [Authorization](06-authorization.md), [Zero Trust IAM](../pam-core/06-zero-trust-iam.md) |
| 5 | The enterprise monitors the integrity and security posture of all assets | No asset is implicitly trusted; posture is measured continuously and checked before access | [PAM Core Capabilities](../pam-core/03-core-capabilities.md) |
| 6 | All resource authentication and authorization are dynamic and strictly enforced before access | Enforcement is an active component (policy decision point / policy enforcement point), not a document | [Authorization](06-authorization.md), [PAM Best Practices](../pam-core/04-best-practices.md) |
| 7 | The enterprise collects as much information as possible and uses it to improve policy | Telemetry feeds policy: sign-in logs, session recordings, and anomaly detection refine access rules | [Incident: Session Review](../scenarios/16-incident-insider-threat-session-review.md), [PAM Architecture](../pam-core/05-pam-architecture.md) |

The short version interviewers and architects use: *never trust, always verify, verify explicitly, and assume breach*. The seven tenets are the formal version of that slogan.

NIST SP 800-207 also names the core logical components of a ZTA: the policy decision point (PDP, which decides) and policy enforcement point (PEP, which blocks or allows), fed by policy information points such as the identity provider, device inventory, and threat intelligence. If you are asked to draw a zero trust architecture in an interview, draw a PDP and a PEP in front of every resource, with the identity provider and asset monitoring feeding decisions. That shape is the whole framework in one picture.

## ISO 27001:2022: the identity controls

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A lists 93 controls in four groups (organizational, people, physical, technological). Identity and access controls are concentrated in the A.5 (organizational) and A.8 (technological) groups. An auditor checks that these controls exist and are evidenced; a security team implements them with the exact tools this site covers.

| Control | Name | What it requires | Where this site covers it |
|---------|------|------------------|---------------------------|
| A.5.15 | Access control | A documented policy for granting, changing, and removing access, based on business and security requirements | [Authorization](06-authorization.md), [IAM Architecture](../protocols-iga/07-iam-architecture.md) |
| A.5.16 | Identity management | Full lifecycle management of unique identities; shared accounts are documented exceptions | [Identity Lifecycle](03-identity-lifecycle.md) |
| A.5.17 | Authentication information | Secure handling of passwords and other authentication secrets, including changes on request and after compromise | [Authentication](05-authentication.md), [Passwordless Authentication](08-passwordless.md) |
| A.5.18 | Access rights | Provision, review, modify, and revoke access rights at defined intervals; prompt revocation on departure | [Identity Lifecycle](03-identity-lifecycle.md), [IGA](../protocols-iga/06-iga.md) |
| A.8.2 | Privileged access rights | Tighter allocation and review of privileged rights, separate from normal user access | [Privileged Account Types](../pam-core/02-privileged-account-types.md), [PAM Best Practices](../pam-core/04-best-practices.md) |
| A.8.3 | Information access restriction | Technical enforcement of need-to-know at the data layer | [Authorization](06-authorization.md) |
| A.8.5 | Secure authentication | Technical authentication controls: MFA, session management, account lockout | [Authentication](05-authentication.md), [MFA Troubleshooting](../scenarios/13-troubleshooting-mfa.md) |
| A.8.18 | Use of privileged utility programs | Restrict and log use of tools with elevated capabilities | [PAM Core Capabilities](../pam-core/03-core-capabilities.md) |

The controls form a chain rather than a list: A.5.15 sets the policy, A.5.16 manages identities, A.5.17 governs how they authenticate, A.5.18 manages what they may do, and A.8.2 through A.8.5 add the technical enforcement. Audit findings in this area are almost always "the policy document exists but the evidence does not", which is exactly where identity tooling earns its budget.

## NIST SP 800-63: authenticator assurance levels

[SP 800-63-4](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html) (current revision, supersedes 800-63-3) defines three authentication assurance levels (AALs) that rank how strong an authentication event is, independent of which product performs it:

| Level | Strength | Phishing resistance | Typical authenticators |
|-------|----------|---------------------|------------------------|
| AAL1 | Basic confidence in a bound authenticator | Not required | Password, single-factor OTP |
| AAL2 | High confidence; two distinct factors required | A phishing-resistant option must be available | Password + TOTP, FIDO2 security key with PIN |
| AAL3 | Very high confidence; hardware authenticator with non-exportable private key | Required | Hardware security key (FIDO2, PIV) |

Two details from the 63-4 revision matter in practice: synchronizable authenticators (passkeys that sync between devices) are permitted at AAL2 but explicitly not at AAL3, and AAL2 now requires verifiers to *offer* a phishing-resistant option rather than merely permitting one. The practical consequence: an organization claiming "AAL2-grade" authentication should be able to show a passkey or security-key enrollment path, not just SMS codes. Full treatment on the [passwordless page](08-passwordless.md).

## How to use frameworks in practice

- **In interviews**, cite the framework, then immediately ground it: "Per SP 800-207 tenet 3, we scoped sessions per resource, which is why we use JIT and short-lived tokens" is a strong answer; "we do zero trust" without a mechanism is not.
- **In audits**, the evidence is the product: access review reports (A.5.18), privileged session recordings (A.8.2), MFA enrollment statistics (A.8.5). If the control exists but no report can be produced, the finding is the evidence gap, not the policy.
- **In architecture**, the frameworks are checklists. When reviewing an IAM design, walk the seven tenets and the identity controls as a rubric: which tenet or control does each component satisfy, and which ones have no answer? The unanswered ones are the roadmap.
